Australian traveller evidence

Winning message: calm arrival, not currency theatre

Lead with “Travel data that behaves itself.”

Australians repeatedly ask how to be connected at the airport, keep their home number for verification SMS, install before departure and avoid misleading “unlimited” fine print. AUD is useful proof of a clear total, but it is not the strongest emotional promise.

Recurring pain points

  1. Daily roaming prices become expensive across a full trip
  2. Setup anxiety and troubleshooting after landing
  3. Keeping the Australian number for bank and account SMS
  4. Confusion about data-only service and local numbers
  5. Throttling, hotspot limits and “unlimited” fine print
  6. Multi-country travel and repeated SIM changes
  7. Foreign-currency and final-checkout uncertainty

Product and copy response

  1. Install before leaving Australia
  2. Keep the Aussie SIM in place and check its roaming rules
  3. State data-only before payment
  4. Admit when a local SIM is better for a long stay
  5. Show full-speed allowance and hotspot rules
  6. Use regional plans for Europe and stopovers
  7. Use dated, expiring competitor claims only

Neon and Vercel

They do different jobs

Vercel runs the product

Vercel builds and serves the website and API routes. It is the application host and deployment system.

Neon remembers what happened

Neon is the managed Postgres database. It stores orders, payment attempts, eSIM state, recovery links, consent, refund cases and privacy requests. Vercel no longer offers its original first-party Postgres product, and its own documentation directs new projects to Marketplace providers such as Neon.

Neon was chosen for Postgres transactions, a serverless driver, Vercel integration and a usable free tier. It complements Vercel rather than replacing it.
